2005: The Year in Quiet Reflection
01/05/2006 01:39:35 AM

I know I'm kinda late with the year-end review thing, but cut me some slack, I'm just now starting to feel like I've got my feet under me. I did a lot of travelling over the holiday season, and for some reason it took me a long time to get my shit back together.

Despite the subject line of this entry, I'm not going to spend a lot of time reviewing 2005. Let me kick it real for a second. 2005 was a great year in some ways, and not a good year in other ways. Pretty mind-blowing statement there, huh? But what I mean is, and please keep in mind that I'm drunk as I write this, 2005 was a CRAZY ASS YEAR for me. And I mean CRAZY ASS year, not crazy ASS YEAR. I don't go in for the ass play. It was a nutty, nutty year. I think...it was the year I started to realize exactly what it is I want out of life. I mean started. Not completed. It's like I just sort of stuck my head out and saw what it was, and I recognized it, but if you asked me to describe it, I couldn't do it. You know?

THREE AWESOME THINGS I LEARNED IN 2005

1. How to drink a gigantic amount of booze without getting hung over the next day.

Two words: clear liquids. Vodka or gin. Minimal mixers. Martinis are best, or tonic water. No fruit juice or super sugary/creamy mixers. This was, for me, a truly revelatory discovery. The thing that, to date, has kept me from becoming a proper drunkard is the bloody hangover, so punishing, so nauseating, that it's hardly even worth getting drunk if you're just going to feel like shit the next day. Maybe if I were a genuine alcoholic, I wouldn't be daunted by the hangover. But I'm not an alcoholic. I'm just a guy who really likes getting drunk. And I'm not one of those lucky bastards with a cast-iron stomach who can slam pitcher after pitcher of beer without feeling like shit. So it's good to find a way to that place where I want to be that doesn't make me feel the next day like a pair of wind-up chattery teeth jammed into a rubber balloon filled with cat vomit.

2. How to not get colds.

The thing that really set 2005 apart from other years is this: I DID NOT GET SICK EVEN ONE DAY DURING 2005. And I don't attribute that to clean living or homeopathic remedies or whatever. I'm as likely to catch a cold now as I was in 2004. Now listen closely because this is probably the most important thing I learned this year. The only way to avoid catching a cold is to avoid exposure to cold viruses. It's that simple. It's not about shoring up your immune system or taking the right herbs or whatever. I read this article that blew my mind, about how, basically, if you're exposed to cold viruses you're going to catch a cold. Period. I'm too soused to look up the actual studies, but they found that pretty darn near everyone who was exposed to cold viruses caught a cold. Which to me is a fascinating factoid, because what it means is that the way to not catch a cold is to...not CATCH THE COLD. You take some basic cold-germ-avoiding precautions, and hope for the best. But here is the most critical piece of information I stumbled across in 2005, which everyone who doesn't want to get sick like a dog should pay heed to:

WHEN TRAVELLING ON AN AIRPLANE, ALWAYS ORDER YOUR BEVERAGE WITHOUT ICE. DO NOT UNDER ANY CIRCUMSTANCES INGEST ICE SERVED ON AN AIRPLANE.

The basis for this rule is a study (again, too soused, look it up yourself) that showed that ice served in drinks on airplanes is UTTERLY FILTHY and riddled with pathogens. We're talking fecal bacteria. Since reading that study, I have specifically asked for beverages sans ice on airplanes, and I've told other people about this, and, get this, ever since they've started following this advice, NO ONE HAS GOTTEN SICK. These are people who used to get sick on a regular basis. So. Take that as you will.

3. Um, something.

How to Not Get Colds (Sober Edition)
01/06/2006 05:45:53 PM

It's been suggested to me that my earlier discussion of avoiding colds was lacking a certain...coherence, so I'm going to take another whack at it, sans gin.

In my view, the most important thing to know about colds, and the thing that most people don't know, is that you cannot "fight off an infection" -- the condition of your immune system makes absolutely no difference as to whether or not you catch a cold in the first place. Even some doctors spread the idea that, by bolstering your immune system, you'll somehow manage to ward off cold germs and keep them from invading your body. This is not true. The study cited shows that about 95% of healthy people exposed to cold viruses do become infected. So, if you think that taking the right herbs or supplements or whatever will keep you from catching a cold, think again. It's true that a healthy immune system can fight the cold back more quickly, but it's not going to prevent you from being infected in the first place. And even among the healthy, 75% of people infected get sick.

So, given that you're basically doomed to infection if you're exposed to the germs, the key becomes to avoid exposure in the first place. Here's my universally acclaimed five-point regimen for not getting colds:

1. Public places are the Casablanca of cold germs. When in public, avoid touching your eyes or nose. These are the primary entry points for the cold germs.

2. Wash your hands often, especially before meals, and carry some sanitizer with you and use immediately after leaving a public place.

3. When flying, always turn off the overhead air nozzle, as this increases air circulation around your seat, increasing the chances of breathing infected air from someone sitting near you.

4. NEVER, EVER, EVER have ice with your in-flight beverage -- the ice you get on airplanes is filthy and germ-ridden. This goes for airplane water in general (coffee, for instance). Drink bottled water only!

5. When staying in hotels, bring some disinfectant cleaning wipes with you, and wipe down the TV remote control (especially important -- this is gross as it's never cleaned by the chambermaids), doorknobs, and faucets. Get rid of the bedspread, because it too is rarely cleaned and usually crazy with germs and other things you don't even want to know about.

Now, I'm not claiming that following this five-point plan will guarantee that you won't get sick, but it will cut your chances way down. Listen to your unca B, you won't regret it!

How Not to Catch Colds Update
01/17/2006 04:48:06 PM

I missed this story when it hit the news sites:

Being cold or chilled really can make you get sick!

They did a study in Wales where they chilled up a bunch of volunteers and then tracked them to see how many got sick versus a control group. 29% of the chilled subjects became sick, versus 9% of the non-chilled group. Yikes!

This is shocking to me because I just recently came around to accepting what all the doctors have been saying, that the "catch your death" thing was just an old wives' tale.

The explanation discussed in the article that makes the most sense to me is that you aren't actually more susceptible to catching a cold when you're chilled, but that the cold causes blood vessels in the nose to constrict, preventing white blood cells from reaching areas that might be "lightly" infected, thus allowing symptoms to develop.

This is why it's important not to tell your friends to "chill out" when they're upset, because you're actually just encouraging them to lower their body temperatures to levels that could lead to serious illness. And why freedom of speech is more important than ever, because if infringements upon that freedom lead to a "chilling effect" on speech, speech could become more susceptible to diseases such as avian flu, in which case we're all screwed. Stay warm, folks.

An Intimate Evening of From-the-Gut Indie Acoustic Music
01/19/2006 01:49:35 AM

Miss H and I went to Indie Coffee to see Jason Anderson (aka Wolf Colonel), with Chris Otepka (aka The Heligoats featuring Chris Otepka, formerly of Troubled Hubble, honestly I have no idea what these names signify, if you do you are truly hipper than I). Neither of us was familiar with these fellows or their music; Miss H, who has an unerring psychic ability to detect and root out awesome-ass live music (but not awesome ass-live music), was the one who suggested this jaunt. We went super early because I have a pathological fear of places to where I have not before been, so I have to go early and scope out the situation. Indie Coffee is super tiny -- it looks like this:



I was dubious upon entering this coffee house, because #1, the place looked like it seated about 10 people, and #2, there didn't seem to be any space for anyone to play. It was explained to us by the affable gentleman behind the counter that a table and two cushioned chairs near the entrance would be removed prior to the performance -- bittersweet, since even though my concerns were addressed, the two cushioned chairs to be taken away were the ONLY SEMI-COMFORTABLE CHAIRS IN THE ENTIRE HOUSE. The others were tiny metal Euro styled things that can probably be found in the Ikea catalog, named TØRTUR.



Coffee was purchased, along with 2 buttermilk waffles which were delicious. We waited and talked and waited, and moved tables three times for better music-spectating positioning (we are strategically minded, Miss H and I).

Waited, talked, waited. Did I mention we arrived super early?

Annoyance as the 8pm show time came and went. The guys showed up around a half hour or so late, and then spent another, I dunno, 15 minutes or something getting ready (for solo, unamplified acoustic sets?). I got kind of antsy and considered raising the possibility of leaving, but then the show started and I was glad we didn't leave.

The Heligoats featuring Chris Otepka played first. The Heligoats featuring Chris Otepka is actually just one person, Chris Otepka, which is trés droll. If I started a band I think I would call it the New Seoul University Choir and Orchestra, and it would just be me and a Powerbook. Anyway, it was good stuff, the kind of charmingly low-key indie singer-songwriter music that I was expecting. Chris seems like a very modest and unassuming sort. Some people came in during a song about Lady Luck and had to walk right past him to sit down, which broke his concentration and he had to stop and start the song over, but he was pretty nice about it.

Then Jason Anderson (aka Wolf Colonel) came on, and whoa! The guy is a madman. First of all, what is with this Wolf Colonel stuff. While waiting for the show, I drew a picture in Miss H's journal of what I thought Wolf Colonel would look like. The picture looked sort of like this:



The actual Wolf Colonel, aka Jason Anderson, looks like this:



Jason Anderson is an insane insaniac...there were maybe 30 or 40 people in this tiny little cramped space that normally holds half of that, and instead of playing up at the front of the house in the designated performance space like a NORMAL SENSITIVE INDIE FOLK-ROCK MUSICIAN he waded into the thick of the crowd, which was kind of vexing to me at first, since that put him about four feet away from me, and I don't normally like to sit that close to sensitive indie folk-rock musicians.

I should have known this was going to be kind of a weird night, given this puzzling description of Anderson I read in the Isthmus:
Pitchfork calls Jason Anderson (aka Wolf Colonel) "almost pathologically earnest." A recent review goes on to say: "His is the rare personality where a Zen-like awareness of the sanctity of the moment, an unremitting gratitude for the gift of existence, and an ostensibly genuine capacity to love strangers are combined into a sort of super-empathy that verges on the grotesque." (Read the full review @ http://www.pitchforkmedia.com/record-reviews/a/anderson_jason/wreath.shtml) This'll be an intimate evening of from-the-gut indie acoustic music.
From-the-gut, for sure. Talk about intense. With acoustic guitar in hand he kicked off the festivities with a medley of 80's/90's covers including such enduring indie folk-rock classics as "In the Air Tonight" by Phil Collins and that one Spin Doctors song. RAWK! Having thoroughly warmed up the audience, he launched into an amazing, hyper-energetic set, jumping around (amazingly, given the close quarters, without bashing anyone in the face) and commanding the puzzled, wonder-struck crowd to sing along with his lively, funny/wise/moving songs. Tongue-in-cheek childhood reminisces (my favorite was a long, meandering monologue about a video from the 80's with cheesy pop songs playing over footage of basketball superstars doing their thing, that finally segued into a raucous group rendition of "The Warrior" by Scandal) shared the plate with uplifting pep talks on surviving Bush's America.

(Not that things got especially political. Though Mr. Wolf Colonel is clearly no neo-con, he seems far more concerned with the spiritual health of his audience than its political affiliations.)

I want to describe Anderson as being like a demented kindergarten teacher conducting a sing-a-long, but that would make his performance sound like a contrived stage gimmick, which it very much wasn't (or didn't seem like), and anyway I already used that description in my entry about the Decemberists concert last October. (I guess demented kindergarten teachers conducting sing-a-longs are the new black.)

Instead, let me say that, as Anderson himself put it, the show was very much in the spirit of folk music, with the musician playing down on the floor with the audience, drawing the people into the music instead of just shouting at them from a raised stage. It's the kind of show that makes me understand what people mean when they talk about the intimacy of live music in small venues. In a larger crowd I'm really only aware of the audience as this undulating mass of nodding heads and amorphous waves of applause and shouting. At this venue, which is the tiniest I've seen to date, I was aware of everyone in the room, could see the amusement and delight on their faces, hear individual laughs and utterances.

I can see why Anderson likes to perform this way. Besides the fact that he's obviously a demented über-extrovert, you can really tell that he's drawing amazing energy from that connection he forms with the audience. For someone like Anderson it's obviously not enough merely to bathe in mass adoration...it must be far more appealing to have some sense of the individuals in the audience and an awareness that these are real, actual people watching you play and singing along with you and giving you their love. Yeah woo!

The sad part for me is that I don't think I appreciated his performance as I'm sure many others do. Not that I didn't enjoy his personality or his songs or his performance style, but I'm too shy and Asperger-y to fully relax in the presence of a musician who's playing, like, RIGHT IN FRONT OF MY FACE. I think that's why, whenever I'm in close proximity to a famous person, I get the urge to punch them. The nearness, the forced intimacy, makes me super nervous and want to strike out at the source of the anxiety. Still, I really liked Anderson and his endearingly earnest, wacked-out, Zen-y message, which is to enjoy and appreciate life in all its messy, random, dorky, goofy craziness, and to not hide behind irony and hip posturing and other forms of fear-of-intimacy. I hope someday I can get to where he's at.

2. Lazy Science Reporting.

NYT: Men enjoy seeing bad guys punished, women don't. In this study they found that when witnessing people "perceived as bad guys" receive electrical shocks, male brains lit up in the pleasure centers, whereas female brains did not. Also, female brains showed more empathy activity. That's fine, but the question is, how do they define "bad" behavior?

This study revolved around a money-based strategy game, where the "bad" players were the ones who behaved selfishly and hoarded money. It seems like sloppy science to make broad generalizations about men and women based on this test. All it seems to show is that men are more measurably outraged by financially selfish behavior than women, not that men necessarily are more vindictive or vengeful. You could as easily conclude that women's maternal, nurturing instincts make them more tolerant of people who behave in childish, self-interested ways. Or that women unconsciously like people who hoard money. Who knows? There's so much going on here, it's ridiculous to reduce the data to one simplistic conclusion.

The study would have been more interesting if they had used a variety of "bad" behaviors. What if the "bad guys" had been shown to be abusing animals or children? Or cheating on a spouse? What if the "bad guy" were a child? The last time I read a news story about animal cruelty, the most bloodthirsty responses I read were from women, by a wide margin.

Maybe men are more vindictive than women, but this study doesn't even really show that. But hey, don't let that get in the way of a juicy water-cooler-chat-friendly headline. Conclusion: meh!

Criminal
02/08/2006 01:20:41 AM



Dave has an interesting entry about digital theft that got me thinking. I'm a criminal, of course. I have never denied it, or argued that illicit file sharing is somehow not illegal. I disagree with the law, but I don't pretend that illicit file sharing, which I'm going to call IFS from now on because I'm just that lazy, is in any way justifiable from a legal standpoint. I don't subscribe to the argument that you aren't "really" stealing something if it's still there after you "take" it. That might be true from a philosophical perspective, but it doesn't jibe with the laws on the books, which is how we're defining theft. So.

What I will say is that, legality aside, I have no personal moral problem with IFS, or to be more precise, IFS as I practice it. My argument:

(1) The entertainment industry has not lost a penny from me due to IFS. If I want to listen or watch or play something badly enough to spend money on it, I do so. If I don't want it that badly, but I'm kind of curious to try it, and I find it online, I'll download it. It's not like I bought more albums back when I couldn't download them. My overall buying habits haven't changed -- if I really want something, I still buy it. I have not spent less money on entertainment than before I discovered the seedy underworld of IFS.

(2) The entertainment industry has actually made more money from me since the advent of IFS. IFS is the only reason I bought any music at all in 2005. Back in, say, 1998, pretty much the only music I listened to was stuff I had on CD from my music-buying days (namely, college, when I actually had disposable income). My music purchases averaged about .5 albums per year. I just wasn't that big of a music fan, and wasn't really interested in or knowledgeable about new bands. It's only since I got into IFS that I've been exposed to new, and especially non-mainstream and indie, artists and music. IFS turned me into a music fan, and since then I've been legitimately buying about 4-5 albums a year, which is what, a 700% increase? I guess I should be against IFS for making me spend more money!

(3) IFS is good for smaller, independent artists. I can understand the RIAA being anti-IFS, because the mainstream music industry depends on a top-down strategy of deciding which music it wants us to hear, and shoving it down our throats. Illicitly downloading the latest Britney album doesn't help the major labels at all, because everyone already knows who Britney is, and pretty much anyone who's gonna be interested in Britney already wants the new album. Nobody's saying to anybody, "Hey, have you heard of this 'Britney Spears' chick? You've gotta hear this!" The major labels are spending millions on marketing, so they have nothing to gain from the benefits of IFS. But if you're a relatively obscure indie artist, IFS is your friend, because it's free marketing. If you're, say, Devin Davis, you've got very little to lose and much to gain from someone sending your album out to a bunch of people and saying, "This guy is awesome, check this shit out." Any loss in album sales due to downloading is going to be made back in spades from new listeners who never would have even heard of you except from word of mouth, which essentially is what IFS is. What's more, for the smallest of artists, the ones selling their CDs from their websites, the bulk of sales will give 100% of the money directly to the artist, and not to Sony/BMG. IFS ultimately is going to vastly expand the indie slice of the music market pie, as a higher percentage of the average music fan's dollar goes to small labels or artists completely outside the industry -- which has to piss off the RIAA.

(4) IFS is good for smaller, independent artists. Another way that IFS works for indie artists is that the greater exposure nets them greater profits in areas where they stand to make more money, such as live shows and merchandising. When I mentioned spending more dough on CDs earlier, I forgot to add that the additional money I'm spending on albums is dwarfed by how much more money I'm spending on live shows. Between 1990 and 2003, I went to a total of three concerts, spending about $100 total. Over 13 years. Between 2004 and today, I've gone to...crap, I can't even count them all. A lot. I've illicitly downloaded albums, then paid to see those bands live. And between tickets and merchandise and booze concessions, I've spent well over $300 in just the past year. Money that has gone to struggling indie bands, local musicians, and small music venues -- in other words, the people who actually need the financial support of fans. And 100% of the money I've spent is a direct consequence of IFS. I would argue that the money I've spent on indie artists as a result of IFS has done far more good in terms of supporting good, innovative music than the same amount spent on some mainstream industry product.

(5) IFS is revolutionizing the entertainment industry. And in a good way. Artists like Wilco (I tried linking directly to the relevant articles, but the Wired archives are screwy, so I'm linking to the snippets on the search page) have realized that FS (illicit or no) is their friend. It's a given these days that bands will offer free mp3 samples from their albums (or entire albums via streaming) on their websites. For the music industry, IFS constitutes a significant competitor, a real threat to revenue, so for the first time in years they're having to compete by offering value-added products, something I predicted when this FS thing first started. Do you really think that they'd be introducing DualDiscs if they didn't have a fire under their asses to put out something that consumers couldn't just download off the net? The revolution is starting with music because that's where most of the IFS action is now, but the effects will be seen in all entertainment media. And it is being driven by IFS.

(6) Video games deserve to be pirated. (This is kind of the flimsiest part of my argument, but bear with me.) Unless they offer playable demos. Why? Because I can't think of another home entertainment format where you're expected to pay $50 for something that it's very possible you won't like, but if you don't, you can't get a refund (there may be stores that give full refunds on opened, played games, but I don't know of any personally). I recently bought the X-Men 2 game for Xbox, and just didn't like it very much. Had I been able to play some kind of demo, I would have known that I wouldn't like it, and wouldn't have blown $50 on the game. As it is, I'm stuck. I can resell it and make back some of my money, but I can't get a refund on a game I've barely played. By the way, I don't pirate Xbox or PS2 games, but I don't care if others do. Oh, and yeah, I know that you can rent console games. You can rent cars, too, but that doesn't mean dealerships should stop offering test drives. What I'm arguing here is basically a pro-consumer position, that the game industry needs to innovate its selling methods to better serve the customer. Computer game makers put out demos. Console game makers also put out demos, sometimes, but they really ought to be putting out entire DVDs of playable demos, at nominal cost, so we can try out the latest games without spending oodles of money. (For all I know, they're already doing this, but if they are, they're not marketing them very well.)

(7) Downloading TV shows isn't really stealing. At least, no more so than taping or Tivo-ing shows off of TV. This point is especially true of broadcast TV, which is sent out over airwaves for free, and less so of premium-cable shows like those on HBO. Sharing broadcast TV shows is more akin to tape recording songs off the radio and making mix tapes out of them. When you watch a network TV show, you're not buying it in any way. It's the advertisers who are buying it. They then give it away for free in exchange for you watching their commercials. The program itself is just the commercial delivery device. But even with premium-cable shows, calling downloading those programs "stealing" is like calling someone a thief who goes over to his friend's house to watch The Sopranos because he doesn't have HBO. Also note that most IFS files of TV shows suck ass in terms of picture/sound quality, since the video/audio is usually highly compressed. What you're getting, most of the time, is a degraded experience -- just like recording off of the radio. Calling this kind of sharing "stealing" is like saying it's stealing the Mona Lisa to take a Polaroid of it. It's not on the same level as pirating video games, or other forms of digital theft. This is theft defined in only the most technical terms. I currently "steal" several TV shows, but only because I don't have cable/broadcast TV and IFS is the only option for most of these shows. I refuse to pay exorbitant cable rates and wade through hours of crap just to get the handful of shows that I actually want. If I could purchase, for a low fee, a la carte episodes of just the shows I wanted, and download them at a super fast speed and reasonably high quality, I would jump on that. The issue for me, and this applies to music and movies as well as TV, is not to get stuff for free, but to get it for what I consider to be a fair price. For instance, I'll pay matinee, and occasionally full, ticket prices for first-run theatrical movies, because those are still priced at a level that I find acceptable. If matinee prices jumped up to $15, I'd start downloading movies. Right now, for music and TV, the choice is either pay full price, or download. There is a price point at which I would gladly give up IFS and purchase the stuff, but I haven't seen that price yet.

(8) IFS is the future. The industry is fighting a lost battle. The nature of entertainment has already been transformed. I said it when Napster first hit and I'll say it again. The entertainment industry is shifting from a top-down to a bottom-up model. The gatekeepers are falling. And this is a good thing for regular human being type people as opposed to rapacious soulless megacorporations, because it means the industry is becoming more artist-centered instead of media-outlet-centered. Historically, mass duplication and cheap/free distribution have been good for artists. When photography enabled people to buy inexpensive duplicates of paintings in the form of posters and prints, there were many in the art world who thought that it would be the end, that no one would be interested in paintings anymore. Why would anyone buy an original Van Gogh if they could have a reasonable facsimile of it on their wall for $10? And the Xerox machine...boy, that really killed the publishing industry, huh? Well, they're saying the same things now. And what happened with paintings is going to happen with music and other digital media. The original article is going to become more valuable relative to the digital copy. "Real" physical products will gain value and features not available via normal IFS channels (DualDiscs...or, for video, high-definition DVDs). Individual artists will become more significant as talent wins out over corporate marketing, and as the ability to create compelling content becomes more important than any particular piece of content. You give away a few albums, people hear them, fall for you, know that you create stuff they want to hear, and you build an audience, and maybe your next new album, people might buy it if only so they can have it right away, and not have to wait until someone copies and distributes it. Or because it has cool album art (how funny would it be if the digital revolution resulted in a return of the album cover?) or a lyrics booklet, or because it's available in 6.1 surround? There may come a time when musicians will give away all their music online, and their revenue will come from live appearances (when music is free, the musician becomes the focus) and merchandising. The point is that everything about the process of creating and selling art that is not mass-produceable -- namely, the individual artists and their talent -- will become astronomically more significant and desirable as the other aspects become cheaper and more common.

Anyway. That's why I support digital theft. And let me reiterate: I know it's stealing. But it shouldn't be. I sincerely believe that free sharing of content is the best thing for the people I care about -- independent artists and consumers -- and will improve the state of entertainment as a whole. And it's one of those things that will never be legal unless it becomes so widespread that it achieves a de facto state of normalcy. Until it is so routinely done, and the culture changes so much, that any attempts to enforce the law simply fade away out of irrelevance. So, as far as I'm concerned, let digital thievery reign supreme!

(Sorry for the long-ass entry. But I only rant about this topic like, once every few years, so cut me some slack.)

P.S.: It occurs to me that I haven't really addressed the content-theft idea in relation to my own stuff that I produce. And my feeling about that is, and has always been, that anyone is free to take/use anything I write or create, provided that (a) they don't use it for commercial purposes; (b) they don't alter it; and (c) they properly attribute it to me and don't take my name off of it, or pass it off as their own. And that kind of answers the whole "how can it be stealing if it still exists after I take it?" question. The way that you can "steal" something digital, in my opinion, is if you essentially obliterate it by mis-attributing it, or tainting it somehow so that the original artist's work is fundamentally compromised. Like if I write a novel and post it online, and someone takes it and re-offers it under his own name, and if somehow that plagiarized version becomes so popular that it becomes fixed in the public mind as a work by that plagiarist. Or if they take the novel and distribute it under my name, but altered somehow so that it stinks, and forever after I'm associated with "that shitty novel." In essence, every time a work like that is distributed, it's just like you stole a physical object, because that work is, in a way, "destroyed" each time it's downloaded.

P.P.S.: More problematic, and the only significant moral qualm I feel about IFS, is that artists' copyrights are being violated in such a way that their control over their own work is taken away. IFS is not so much a problem when it comes to bands like Wilco that support file sharing, but what about artists who don't want their work offered online? They're being screwed by IFS, regardless of whether or not they ultimately benefit from it, because their wishes are not being honored. Not that they fare any better under the traditional system, anyway, not really, but I don't agree with the traditional system, either. In the end I believe all artists will benefit from the changes wrought by IFS, or at least come out even, but all the same, do we have the right to foist those benefits on artists who don't want them?

Final P.S.: Comments deactivated on this entry, because if there is going to be any discussion at all on this topic, I'd like it to be in the forums.

Real Final P.S.: Something else I just realized. The RIAA argument for criminality basically depends on a closed system -- each album downloaded via IFS equals one lost album sale. That's what defeats the "it's not stolen if it's still there" defense. Even if the digital copy still exists, the download represents one customer who will not be buying the album. But in the real world, it doesn't quite work that way. You have people like me, who weren't planning on buying the album whether it was online or not. I'm not a lost sale. What I am, though, is a potential future sale. I'm the guy in Borders who browses around, finds a book that looks intriguing, and skims through it. If I didn't have that book to skim through, I would never even potentially buy that book, because I very, very rarely buy books merely because of the author, title, or subject. But, because Borders does allow customers to skim through their books, every so often I find a book that I like enough to buy. Or I discover an author whom I dig enough so that I will buy whatever else he/she publishes. So, if the music industry wants me and consumers like me as customers, they'd do well to give us that Borders experience.

V
03/24/2006 06:10:19 PM



This entry contains spoilers.

This is not a review of V for Vendetta, which is great and everyone should go see it now and every week until this November, but merely one man's rambling reflection on one aspect of the film.

V for Vendetta, contrary to appearances, is not a superhero movie. V is not a superhero in the customary sense of the term. He superficially resembles comic book heroes -- the Batman-style secret lair, the masked costume, the use of violence to combat evildoers -- but the nature of the character V is in direct conflict with that of a true superhero.

Here's why:

Superheroes ultimately represent the dominance of order over anarchy. They fight, not merely to vanquish criminals, but to restore society to its proper functioning. Criminality in the superhero universe represents a malfunction in the machine of civilization; superheroes are the mechanics who maintain and repair the machine.

Batman, one of the darkest and most untamed of heroes, is nevertheless a defender of order. He may be a maverick and an outlaw because he won't play by society's rules, but at the end of the day, he's there to help the cops sweep up the trash, not to blow up the garbage truck. Batman kicks the shit out of criminals in the name of justice, but V dismisses Lady Justice pretty early on in the game; V's about revenge, pure and simple. Vendetta. Even the most morally ambiguous superhero I can think of, the Punisher, who demolishes his hapless criminal victims in firestorms of bullets and high explosives, operates out of a need to manage and assuage his limitless grief and rage, to keep himself from imploding. He's insane, but he's trying to harness his insanity, not unleash it.

Watching V for Vendetta, I was struck by how much V reminded me, not of Batman, but the Joker. Like V, the Joker is a victim of, and born out of, modern science, his body and mind disfigured and warped by chemicals. Like the Joker, V is flamboyant, physically nimble and powerful, and most assuredly insane. He even poisons his victims, like Joker does with his rictus-grin-producing venom, and arranges their corpses to shock and horrify the authorities. And of course, like the Joker, V is an agent of chaos and destruction. (Plus: I surely wasn't the only comic book graphic novel geek to sense a parallel between V/Evey and Joker/Harley Quinn?)

My take on V is that he's not a superhero but a superantihero, a dark, scary protagonist intended to disturb and creep us out, even as we root for his V-ictory over the fascist oppressors. He's the twisted progeny of the state, literally created by the very government he sets out to destroy, the mutant chicken coming home to roost. That's why the third-act plot twist is so shocking and yet so appropriate; no hero would do what V does to Evey. That's the critical moment in which, in case it escaped us before, we understand that we are watching a madman, possibly a sociopath -- not unhinged in a safely controlled way, like Batman, but in a scary, capable-of-anything way, like Joker. That's why the V-people he inspires don't go out and fight the power, but rob innocent merchants. That's where I think the book and movie both take their biggest risk -- pushing the protagonist's craziness beyond the limits of comfort that we expect from our heroes, while still asking us to stick with him to the end.

As the film (and V) points out repeatedly, V is the symbolic reincarnation of Guy Fawkes, who, we must remember, is not a hero, but a terrorist, who plotted to blow up the entire government, including the king and both houses of Parliament. On Guy Fawkes Night, Britons don't celebrate Guy Fawkes, but rather the foiling of his plot, and his arrest and execution. This is why V is not like the Punisher, who does what he does in order to survive. V knows he's boarding an express train to his own death. He's not trying to manage his psychosis, but shape it like a plastic explosive, get it ready for the right moment in which to explode, destroying both himself and his enemy.

What, then, is V saying when he quotes the Guy Fawkes rhyme, "Remember, remember the fifth of November"? The answer, I believe, is that V (and through V, the author and the filmmakers) is reminding us that tyranny, fueled by bigotry and intolerance (Fawkes was a Catholic who felt oppressed by the Protestant King John), inevitably gives rise to chaos, violence, and anarchy. V for Vendetta can be read as simple anarchist agit-prop fantasy, but to my eyes it's more like a warning, a cautionary tale for a nation that is perhaps closer to authoritarian dictatorship right now than ever before.

V's future is not our present, but a dystopian nightmare vision of our possible fate. We're not meant, I don't think, to walk out of the movie seething with the urge to foment an armed insurrection. Rather, I believe we're meant to think about how fucked up V's world must have been, to give rise to such a vengeful, ruthless, deadly creature. And then, maybe, to compare that world with our own, and to think about the monsters that have risen up in our own time, created by our own corrupt, short-sighted leaders, and about the destruction our monsters have unleashed.

V's spiritual relations are not comic book heroes, but Travis Bickle, or the monster from Frankenstein. Or maybe guys like John Brown, the 19th century abolitionist who waged guerilla warfare against pro-slavery southerners. Frederick Douglass said of Brown, "John Brown began the war that ended American slavery and made this a free Republic. His zeal in the cause of my race was far greater than mine. I could live for the slave, but he could die for him." The message of V for Vendetta, for me, is that we must do everything we can to prevent our society from falling into a condition that can only be remedied by a bloodbath.

Also, it's pretty sweet looking in IMAX.

Canciones del Amor y de la Tristeza
04/05/2006 02:14:36 AM



The inspiration for this latest volume in the ever-unfolding saga that is B's Quest to Become the Most Redesign-Crazy Weblogger in the History of Weblogging comes from a 1938 book by the late Brenda Ueland, If You Want to Write: A Book about Art, Independence, and Spirit. In her chapter entitled "The Tigers of Wrath Are Wiser Than the Horses of Instruction" (a proverb from William Blake's The Marriage of Heaven and Hell), Ueland, one of the most no-nonsense, spiritually centered writers I've ever read, says this:
For I know that the energy of the creative impulse comes from love and all its manifestations -- admiration, compassion, glowing respect, gratitude, praise, compassion, tenderness, adoration, enthusiasm.

Compare the tenderness of great artists with the attitude of critics towards other men.

"You are kind to painters," Van Gogh wrote to his brother, "and I tell you the more I think, the more I feel that there is nothing more truly artistic than to love people."


Note that Ueland writes "compassion" twice up there. Typo? Maybe, but I doubt it. What she's advocating, I think, in this book, is not carelessness over precision, but love over logic, passion (wrath) over reason (instruction). Reason here being distinguished from understanding, as she quotes Blake:
"What we so often call Reason," Blake said, "is not the Understanding at all but is merely derived from the experience of our five senses, derived from Earth and from our bodies."
Ueland isn't telling us to go crazy or lose control of ourselves. She wants us to simply be who we are, without the self-consciousness, the need to impress, the need for approval, that obstruct our imaginations and stifle the real voice that is within us. It's pretty heady stuff if, like me, you spend an inordinate amount of time caring very much whether or not people think you're a jackass. And it's surprisingly hard to just "be" without a lot of soul-killing analysis of just who or what we "are" -- or maybe more accurately, (just) who we think we "are" (anyway).

I always know I've been honest, and true to myself, in a piece of writing, when I wake up the next morning thinking, "Oh, holy crap, please tell me that stuff I wrote and posted on the Internet last night was just a terrible dream," and "I wonder if I can delete it before anyone actually reads it?" I guess that's something I need to get over at some point, if I want to be a writer of honest, true-to-oneself words. Failing that, there's always the option of just giving up and settling for being a hack, and why does a solidly constructed piece of hackwork not give me the morning-after frisson of horror that I suspect it should?

Most of the time I either don't know what I want to do, or I want to do about five things at once. But maybe that's okay. Here's what Ueland says about consistency: "Later if you find what you wrote isn't true, accept the new truth. Consistency is the horror of the world."

So maybe tomorrow or in a week or three months I'll tire of what I've been doing and want to do something else. But this is what I want to do now. If that's okay with you. Wait -- okay? What? I don't care if it's okay with you. Screw you! No, no, no -- I love you. I love you! See, writing is hard.

A Selection of Excellent Weepies Songs For Your Enjoyment

We saw the Weepies -- Steve Tannen and Deb Talan -- at the bar of the Café Montmartre, ordering food. It must have been good, because later onstage they raved about the chèvre crostini. I'm always wondering why bands that normally play larger venues show up at someplace as relatively small as the "Momo," and now I know: it's the free food. And yeah, it is pretty damn good.

Speaking of food, earlier H and I were at the Willy St. Co-op, eating a bunch of stuff from the über-vegan deli, and we saw Tag getting some über-vegan food. H bemoaned the fact that we are turning into those Madison people who hang out at places like the Co-op and the Momo, and she's right -- we are turning into those people! It's already getting to where I seem to vaguely recognize everyone I see, everywhere I go in this town. I need to either make friends with these people, or move to another city. At the very least, I should probably stop making fun of them, since they may well be reading this weblog.

Speaking of making fun of people, I've decided to stop making "oh my God" eye-rolling expressions at the inane things I overhear people saying while in public spaces. As stultifying as it is to hear people say things like, "I've got like a million pants but I only wear one or two pairs," the fact is that most of the things we say to each other sound pretty lame to strangers.

The thing that turned me around on this matter is when H was wondering what kind of shoes Deb Talan was wearing, and I was all like, "Uh....maybe they're those shoes...you know...like...that hippies wear." I may have added "and stuff" at the end, for good measure. And I thought, you know what, if I heard someone saying what I just said, I'd want to whack them in the head. The only two possibilities open to me, then, are to either stop saying inane things myself, and to ensure that every word out of my mouth is witty and/or brilliant, or stop getting annoyed by other people's inane utterances. Since the first option isn't actually all that open to me, I guess I'm stuck with the second.

Poo.


If anyone can identify these shoes (big, bulbous, rubbery-looking, with a sort of backward tilt), please contact me.


One thing I've got to interject here is that, if the Momo didn't book such awesome musical acts, we would so not ever go there. The service is uneven, and in a way that is entirely mysterious. They seem to be in a state of continuous motion, yet nothing ever seems to get served. Even when the place is nearly deserted, the level of service and quality of product is indicative of a packed house. I mean, if we're at Dotty Dumpling's Dowry, normally the home of the greatest hamburgers in the contiguous United States, during a busy lunch hour, I don't expect super-prompt service or impeccably-cooked food, especially when there are maybe two or three waitresses trying to serve around 40-50 people. But when you've got two or three servers and maybe twelve patrons, is it unreasonable to expect to get a menu or something within the first quarter hour of your visit? Yeesh! There is at least one bartender and one waiter there who's reliably attentive, but unless you're lucky you're in for a really strange sort of experience, where you might get good service, or maybe not, depending on, I don't know, the whims of the staff and the alignment of the planets.

Seriously, this is the kind of thing that leads to Noodles & Company.

Also, I don't know if this is part of the Momo's bohemian art-jazz aesthetic, but I have questions about their policy of leaving the front door wide open to sub-freezing winds during the winter, but shutting it tight during the warm weather (needless to say, the place has no detectable air conditioning). The Weepies commented on the stifling heat a couple of times during their set, so it's clearly a problem. I'm not sure what the deal is here.

I think I'm done bitching about the Momo now. Wait, let me check...yep, all done.



The opening act was Josh Harty, a Madison folk singer/songwriter. I liked his stuff -- kind of bluesy, with gospel influences -- but I asked H later how she liked it, she made sort of a face. I don't think H is much of a blues fan.



The Weepies are all about the sweet-sounding pop-folk. I had no idea who they were until we saw Tag putting up that Weepies poster and checked them out. I felt it was a sign that we should see them when one of their songs showed up in Friends with Money, a movie we really liked. I thought, "Wow, these guys are totally on the cusp of global domination. We must see them now when they're relatively untainted by success and we can actually hear them in a tiny club for like eight bucks."

Steve Tannen and Deb Talan are married, and initially met through their music, as fans of each other's work, which is pretty much the ultimate artist's fairy tale romance. You can really tell that they're into each other as musicians and as people; their onstage chemistry is terrific. They're the kind of artists I really like, who exude their love of what they're doing. Steve remarked a couple of times that this was a dream come true for him. They just seem like such nice kids, gosh darn it, and you've gotta have a hard heart indeed not to root for them.



Afterwards, as we staggered out of the Momo towards the car, which, mercifully, was downhill from the café, I felt a profound sense of contentment. Perfect springtime music for a perfect springtime evening.

Yogurt Experiment
06/16/2006 10:58:33 AM

Devendra Banhart, "I Feel Just Like A Child"


While I'm not much of a yogurt eater generally, there's one yogurt I really love -- Stonyfield Farm Organic Whole Milk Yogurt. It's organic, it's the only yogurt I can find around here that is made with whole milk, so it's creamier, and there is in fact a rich layer of cream at the top, which you can eat or mix into the yogurt (I eat the cream layer by itself).

If you get the fruit variety (there are two -- Wild Blueberry and Strawberries & Cream), the fruit is at the bottom like in many brands of yogurt. You stir it before eating, drawing the glutinous fruit paste up into the larger body of the yogurt in a satisfying swirl of cheery fruit colors. (I guess you can also eat the yogurt and fruit portions separately, but that's a perversion I choose not to get into in this entry.)



Today I'm eating French Vanilla, which, as you might have guessed by the "French" and "Vanilla," is not a fruit-based flavor. So the question which occurs to me is, in a non-fruit-based yogurt, is the flavored portion still at the bottom?

Let's find out!

First of all, we must open up the tub and extract the delicious cream layer. Mmm, creamy.



Having done that, I insert the spoon into the faintly gelatinous yogurt, careful not to disturb any flavor strata that might lurk within. Since I don't have the tools on hand to obtain a proper core sample, I have to sort of push aside the top 75% or so of the yogurt to bring up a spoonful of yogurt material from the bottom.



At first glance, the sample extracted from the bottom of the tub does not appear to be any different from the rest of the yogurt material. I taste the sample, detecting a distinct vanilla flavor but not the kind of concentrated flavor one might expect if all the vanilla flavor were at the bottom. Tasting a sample from the top of the tub confirms my initial observation: there is no detectable difference between the top portion of the yogurt and the bottom portion.

I should also note that, in the time it has taken to actually taste it, the yogurt has gotten warm and runny, and is kind of gross.

Therefore, my conclusion is that, at least in the vanilla configuration, the flavored portion is not on the bottom, but fully integrated into the whole of the yogurt.
